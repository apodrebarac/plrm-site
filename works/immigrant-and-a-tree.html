Work 07

Immigrant and a Tree

Year 2019 — Ongoing
Location London
Medium EEG, data mapping, poetry performance, walking
Status Ongoing

Non-native trees of Britain — brought here through colonial endeavours — do not exist in the British landscape. They survive.

This work came from observing trees on daily walks across London. Its philosophical insights come from the interconnectedness of life, approached through Gilles Deleuze's concept of the rhizome — a non-hierarchical, networked model of being and knowledge that perfectly summarises the investigation into the life of trees, particularly those in Britain that bear the invisible marks of colonial histories.

During walks across the city, non-native trees — those that do not belong in Britain but were brought here during the Empire era — were mapped. Katherine Hayles's insights into the complexity at all levels of life and the translation of embodied knowledge into systematic constructions guided this research into their connections. Trees that do not belong to the same family do not communicate through the fungal network; they are disconnected from one another — a tribal mentality in foreign territory that uses one another only for resources.

The response was to create a pattern from mapping these trees around London, transforming this data into poetic texts. These poems are not literary outputs but performances that seek to re-establish lost communications — not only between the trees but also between disparate cultures and histories. In performing these poems to the trees, a dialogic act is engaged in: attempting to bridge the communicative gaps imposed by colonial histories.

EEG integration

This work extends into EEG brain signal recording during the walks and performances. The electrical activity of the brain encountering these displaced trees becomes another layer of data — the body's own response to non-belonging mapped alongside the trees' displacement. The signals are assigned words ("o" and "1"), generating a further poetic layer that interweaves neural data with arboreal geography.

Theoretical framework

This performative act is rhizomatic in its essence: it creates connections where none visibly exist, proposing a new language for understanding and relating to nonhuman and colonised lives. This work advocates for a world where multiple points of connection and interaction are recognised and valued, fostering a more inclusive and interconnected understanding of our shared world.
